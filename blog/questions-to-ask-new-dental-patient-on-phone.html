What Should a Dental Office Ask a New Patient on the Phone?

Ask for the name, how they heard about the office, the reason in their own words, whether they consider it urgent, the insurance carrier and subscriber details needed to verify coverage, preferred times, and how to reach them. Get consent before texts. Book a time before you hang up, and leave the medical history for the forms.

This page is for the practice owner or office manager writing the script for a first call. Whoever picks up needs the same short list, and a time on the schedule before the caller hangs up.

What should a dental office ask a new patient on the first call?

Ask for eight items, write the answers in the caller's words, and stop. The call is done when a visit is on the schedule, or when the note says who called, why, and why a time was not booked.

Use the same order every time. A note that says only "new patient, call back" has no reason, no insurance details, and no window that matches an open chair.

Ask Write this down
Name Caller's name, spelled back. Patient's name too, if someone else is calling.
How they heard about the office Their words. Do not rename the source.
Reason for the visit Their words. Do not turn the phrase into a diagnosis.
Whether they consider it urgent Yes or no, plus their phrase. Then the office's urgent-care process, or 911.
Insurance for verification Carrier, member ID, and date of birth. Subscriber details if the plan is in someone else's name.
Preferred times Two or three windows inside hours the office is open.
How to reach them The number or email they name.
Text consent, if the office texts Yes or no. Ask before you send.

Spell the name back. If the caller is booking for someone else, write both names and who the visit is for. Keep "how they heard about the office" and the reason in their words. "It hurts when I chew on the left" is the note. Do not rename it, and do not promise what the visit will find.

If they say it is urgent, follow the office's own urgent-care process. If they describe a life-threatening emergency, tell them to call 911.

What insurance details does the first call need?

Collect the carrier, the member ID, and the date of birth while the caller is still on the line. If the plan is in someone else's name, also collect the subscriber's information and the patient's details.

Verifying insurance during the call means reading that result back before they hang up. For a child, collect the subscriber's information and the dependent's details in the same conversation. The office managers page says the result can include deductibles and remaining benefits.

Run the check only when the practice has turned verification on, and only for plans the office accepts. If the carrier is not one you take, say so and offer the next step the office already uses. Do not guess what a plan pays. A question the written answers do not cover goes back to the staff with the note attached.

What should the front desk leave off the phone?

Leave the detailed medical history off the call. Medications, conditions, and the rest of the health history belong on the office's forms.

A spoken list is easy to mis-hear, and it holds the line while the next caller waits. The call books the visit. The form holds the history.

  • A clinical opinion, including what a symptom probably is.
  • A promise about what the dentist will do at the visit.
  • A fee you have not looked up on the office's own fee list.

If the caller asks a clinical question — pain, medication, or care after a procedure — write it in their words and send it to the practice. Do not answer it from the script. "They consider it urgent" is a flag for the office's own urgent-care process, not a diagnosis. A life-threatening emergency is 911.

How do you close the call with a time on the schedule?

Offer a real opening, repeat it, and hang up only after the caller has heard the day and the time. "Someone will call you back" is the close only when the visit cannot be booked from the rules you already have.

  1. Offer two times inside their windows, your open hours, and the providers who see new patients. Do not offer a slot the schedule does not have.
  2. Repeat the patient's name, the day, the time, and what to bring: insurance card, photo ID, and the form if you use one.
  3. Read back the number or email they named.
  4. If you text, ask first and write down the answer. The FCC's consumer guide says autodialed texts to a mobile phone need prior consent, unless the message is for an emergency. Commercial texts need written consent. For informational texts, consent may be oral.
  5. If you cannot book, say why in one sentence. Write who called, why, and what you told them, and who follows up.

Then stop. "New patient, please call back" is not a finished call. The next person cannot book from it, and the caller never heard a time.

How do you capture the same details when the front desk cannot answer?

Use the same eight questions when the desk cannot pick up. After hours, lunch, and a busy line are different gaps, and the list does not change.

After-hours answering is the nights-and-weekends coverage: book a time, or write who called, why, and what they were told. A name-and-number recording misses the reason, the insurance details, and a preferred time. Lunch uses the same list. Frequently asked questions

What should a dental office ask a new patient on the phone?

Ask for the name, how they heard about the office, the reason in their own words, whether they consider it urgent, the insurance carrier and subscriber details needed to verify coverage, preferred times, and how they want to be reached. If the office sends texts, ask for consent and write down the answer. Book a time before you hang up, and leave the detailed medical history for the forms.

What insurance information belongs on the first call?

The carrier, the member ID, and the date of birth. If the plan is in someone else's name, also take the subscriber's information and the patient's details. That is the set used to check eligibility during the call, including when a parent calls for a dependent.

Should the front desk take a medical history on the phone?

No. The detailed medical history belongs on the office's forms. The phone call is for booking. A clinical question, including pain, medication, or care after a procedure, is written down and sent to the practice. Do not answer it from the script.

What if the caller says the visit is urgent?

Follow the office's own urgent-care process. Do not give clinical advice, and do not decide the clinical problem on the call. If the caller describes a life-threatening emergency, tell them to call 911.

How is this different from coverage when the front desk is slammed?

This page is the question list for the first call, whoever answers. The slammed front desk post is about overflow when the office is open and the desk is already full. Use that post for the coverage path. The questions here stay the same.

Can Aria collect this list when the front desk cannot pick up?

Yes.
